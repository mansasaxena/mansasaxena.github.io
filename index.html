Distorted Signals and Evaluator Bias: A Two-Stage Experiment on Gendered Hiring in Malawi

with Gabriella Fleischman

PDF

Female labor force participation is constrained by supply-side frictions, including a lack of qualified female applicants, and demand-side frictions, including biases in hiring decisions. We examine how these distortions interact to affect gender gaps in employment through two experiments conducted in partnership with a firm. The first uses female-directed advertising during a recruitment drive to address supply-side behavioral gender gaps; the second uses a résumé audit study to isolate evaluator biases in hiring. Although the intervention does not affect applicants' objective skills, it unexpectedly reduces female hiring. We find that evaluators place greater weight on noisy signals of soft skills for women than for men, while the recruitment intervention induces lower-ability women to inflate their soft-skill signals. Consequently, evaluators shortlist lower-ability female candidates from treated areas, crowding out higher-ability women. Our estimates suggest that addressing either distorted applicant signals or evaluator bias would close the gender gap in the ability of shortlisted candidates by 51–55%, with only modest additional gains from addressing both.

Inspiring Female Micro-Entrepreneurs to Adopt Mobile Money in Pakistan

with Essoyodou Marie Laure Pello Sode and Mahniya Zafar

Pilot complete

Project Page

Digital financial technologies can lower transaction costs and expand access to financial services, yet their adoption and productive use remain limited among women entrepreneurs. We examine whether practical information about mobile money and exposure to relatable female entrepreneurs who successfully use it in their businesses can increase adoption and use. We pilot these interventions with 299 female micro-entrepreneurs affiliated with a microfinance institution in Lahore, Pakistan. Client groups were randomly assigned to receive either an informational intervention explaining how to use mobile money, with optional hands-on assistance, or the same intervention supplemented by a role-model video featuring successful female entrepreneurs from the same network. Providing information increased registration of mobile money accounts in women's own names by 15 percentage points, relative to a control-group mean of 21%, and reduced perceived fraud risk. Combining information with a role model increased business-related mobile money use by 11 percentage points and transactions with customers or suppliers by 9 percentage points. These findings suggest that informational barriers constrain initial adoption, while trust and perceived benefits may shape subsequent business use.
